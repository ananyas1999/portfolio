Dynamic Hotel Pricing Engine

Dynamic Hotel Pricing Engine

Project Motivation
In the post-COVID hospitality landscape, hotels have been aggressively trying to recover lost revenue while managing unpredictable booking patterns. During my internship, I observed that many hotels still relied on static pricing models that ignored crucial real-time variables like local events, seasonality, weather, and competitor pricing. I wanted to build a system that could dynamically adjust prices using historical and real-time data, helping hotel managers make smarter revenue decisions. The idea was to build an intelligent, data-driven hotel pricing engine that could analyze seasonality, demand trends, and competitive rates to optimize room pricing.

My Role
I independently conceptualized and executed this project from scratch. I was responsible for the entire data science pipeline including data sourcing, cleaning, model building, testing, visualization, and final deployment of insights via Tableau. I took the role of a full-stack data analyst and data scientist here, playing product owner, data engineer, and analyst simultaneously.

Tools & Technologies Used
• Python (pandas, scikit-learn, statsmodels) for regression analysis and time series forecasting.
• Tableau for interactive data visualization and real-time pricing dashboards.
• Jupyter Notebook for experimentation and model tracking.
• Excel as a reference point for early testing and validating pricing scenarios manually.

I chose Python because of its vast libraries for machine learning and its flexibility in data preprocessing. I used Tableau for its simplicity and powerful drill-down capabilities that hotel managers could use without deep technical knowledge.

Alternative Tools Considered
I briefly evaluated Power BI for visualization, but Tableau’s drag-and-drop interface and time series forecasting capabilities gave it an edge for this use case. I also considered building the model in R (using forecast and prophet), but Python allowed better integration with the data cleaning and wrangling pipeline. For more complex use cases, tools like Google OR-Tools or advanced reinforcement learning systems could be used, but they were too heavy for this specific goal.

Approach & Process

  • Data Collection: Gathered historical pricing data from public hotel booking APIs, scraped competitor pricing from online travel agencies, and incorporated weather/event schedules.
  • Preprocessing: Cleaned missing values, handled outliers using IQR method, and created dummy variables for categorical factors like season, location, and room type.
  • Feature Engineering: Engineered lag variables, applied rolling averages to smooth trends, and created pricing bands for day-of-week and event proximity.
  • Modeling: Built multivariate linear regression model for transparency. Experimented with ARIMA and Exponential Smoothing for comparison. Used cross-validation RMSE for model selection.
  • Evaluation: Final regression model had an R² of 0.87. Deviation from optimal prices was within 6% on a holdout set.
  • Dashboarding: Designed an interactive Tableau dashboard with filters for seasonality, events, and occupancy. Included sliders to simulate competitor impact and demand levels.

Results & Impact
• Revenue projections improved by ~14% over traditional static pricing models.
• Managers could simulate pricing strategies visually and react to changing demand.
• Reduced time spent on manual spreadsheet updates from 4 hours/week to 15 minutes using the dashboard.

Challenges Faced
• Scraping hotel booking sites involved rate limits, requiring retry logic.
• Sparse or incomplete event data forced me to create proxy variables (e.g., sports season indicators).
• Translating complex model outputs into simple visual insights took multiple user interviews and dashboard redesigns.

Lessons Learned
• Even the best model is useless without user trust—visualization and explainability matter.
• Pricing strategies must account for both high-demand surge and low-demand discounting.
• Early user feedback improves usability—my dashboard improved significantly after mock interviews with hotel staff personas.

Conclusion
This project taught me how to go from raw data to actionable business tools. It helped bridge the gap between analytics and operations by delivering insights in a form hotel teams could actually use. The system is scalable and can be extended to other dynamic pricing domains like ride-hailing, airfares, or event ticketing. Above all, it reminded me that data science isn’t just about models—it’s about enabling decisions.

Marketing Funnel Optimization 🍜

Let Me Paint a Picture
Imagine a bustling cloud kitchen in Koramangala, Bangalore. It’s Friday night. Orders are flying in through Zomato and Swiggy. But here’s the twist: while 10,000 people view the menu, only 500 actually place an order. Somewhere between Add to Cart and Place Order, interest evaporates.

That’s what many marketing funnels feel like. Clicks come in. Impressions look good. But conversions? Meh. It’s like prepping 1,000 biryanis and watching 900 go cold. This project was about finding where the heat is lost, and fixing it.

What I Set Out To Do
Not just make a dashboard with KPIs. I wanted to build a full optimization system-one that:

  • Uncovered hidden funnel drop-offs
  • Quantified the exact "why" behind user falloff
  • Let teams experiment like a kitchen trying new recipes-and see results before serving them to customers

My Role
I was the data chef here-from strategy to plating. I sliced through noise, simmered data models, and served insights that teams could actually act on. From cleaning the data pantry to garnishing the final Power BI dashboard, this was my kitchen.

Tools I Used (and Why)
• Power BI: Fast and user-friendly for non-tech teams-perfect for exec reviews.
• Python (pandas, sklearn, scipy): To run A/B tests, build regressions, and simulate behavior changes.
• Excel: Quick validations and budget impact models.

I skipped Tableau for this one-Power BI just meshed better with the team’s daily tooling.

Recipe for Funnel Optimization

  • Awareness → Consideration → Intent → Conversion: Broke the funnel into these 4 stages
  • Defined KPIs for each: bounce rate, CTA clicks, conversion delays
  • Ran A/B tests to refine UX-like CTA timing and design
  • Used logistic regression for conversion prediction
  • Simulated "What if we did X?" scenarios for forecasted impact

Results That Stuck
• 28% improvement in ROI prediction accuracy
• Real-time simulation of what-if changes
• 15% increase in conversions by repositioning CTAs
• Redirected budget from low-performing campaigns → 15% increase in quality leads

Bonus Discoveries
• Mobile users behaved differently-CTAs needed a different layout
• Fear-based CTAs ("Last chance!") underperformed in health & wellness campaigns

What I Learned
• Funnels are behavioral systems-not just dashboards
• Great experiments fail if no one understands the results
• Blend data with design to tell stories teams can believe in

Looking Ahead
I’m expanding this to:

  • Multi-channel funnel analysis: Email + SMS + In-app
  • UX heuristics + heatmap insights
  • Causal modeling to get closer to "why" behind behavior

This wasn’t just analytics-it was behavioral economics in a Bangalore-sized cloud kitchen. And now, when I look at a leaky funnel, I don’t just patch it-I ask, “What ingredient is missing in the recipe?”

Fraud Detection Engine for Credit Card Transactions

Why I Built This

This project started when I experienced a real credit card fraud incident in my family. One of my relatives had an unknown international transaction go through, and it wasn’t flagged. The bank only noticed two days later. That moment made me ask myself: how do fraud systems work, and why do they sometimes miss obvious patterns? I wanted to see if I could train a machine learning model to detect fraud better and faster.

What I Actually Built

I built a fraud detection engine using two models:

  • XGBoost for supervised learning on labeled transaction data
  • Isolation Forest for anomaly detection based on unusual patterns
I used a dataset with over 280,000 transactions where only a small percentage were fraud. This was very realistic and made the problem more challenging.

Why XGBoost, Not Random Forest?

I actually tested both. Random Forest gave decent accuracy, but:

  • It struggled more with class imbalance
  • Training took longer
  • It had a higher rate of false positives
XGBoost worked better because:
  • It handled imbalanced data more effectively
  • It allowed weighting of the fraud class
  • It reduced false positives by about 34 percent compared to Random Forest

How I made it the best?

I didn’t just build a basic model. I:

  • Split the data into training and test sets
  • Used SMOTE and undersampling to balance the classes
  • Tuned XGBoost parameters using grid search
  • Measured performance using precision, recall, and F1-score
  • Combined XGBoost with Isolation Forest for dual-layer detection

Results

  • Recall improved by 34 percent compared to baseline Random Forest
  • False positives were significantly reduced
  • The system flagged potential fraud cases within seconds

What I Learned

  • Fraud detection is about catching the right things, not everything
  • XGBoost is powerful but needs proper tuning to avoid overfitting
  • Class imbalance requires more than just a good algorithm, it needs smart sampling and careful evaluation

That idea stayed with me. I decided to build a planning system tailored specifically for internal teams to monitor projects and KPIs in one place.

Inspiration

Tools I Used

• Cloud Composer to automate data loading every morning before the workday.

What It Actually Did

  • Integrated 12+ team-specific spreadsheets into one consistent data model.
  • Created alerts for overdue tasks, stuck projects, and missing owners.

Outcomes

Streaming Order Analytics Platform

This project started with a simple question. What if we could track orders as they happen in real time instead of waiting for end-of-day reports? That idea pushed me to create a streaming data pipeline that connects all the way from the point of sale to a dashboard anyone can understand.

I used Apache Kafka to capture every new order event as soon as it occurred. These events were processed and pushed to AWS Redshift using Airflow. From there, I created SQL views and Tableau dashboards that helped different teams monitor things like sales delays, region-wise performance, or peak order times with just a glance.

The dashboards gave near real-time visibility and helped the operations team act quickly. If a region was falling behind on deliveries or a product's sales dropped unexpectedly, they could catch it instantly instead of discovering it days later.

I learned that data doesn’t just need to be accurate. It has to be timely and simple to act on. This project taught me how crucial speed is in data pipelines and how tools like Kafka and Airflow help turn data into quick decisions.

Why I Built This

Inspiration
Most traditional data systems struggle to keep up with the speed and size of modern retail data. I was inspired to build something that could efficiently store raw data, process it quickly, and deliver actionable insights — all without needing expensive hardware or legacy infrastructure.

• AWS S3 to store large volumes of retail data in a flexible, scalable way.
• Apache Spark for batch data processing and cleaning.
• Athena to query data directly from S3 using SQL.
• Terraform to spin up and configure all AWS resources automatically.
• Power BI to present insights with filters for time, region, and product category.

What It Actually Did

  • Processed and analyzed 1M+ product-level transactions from mock retail datasets.
  • Revealed clear sales patterns by season and product category, using interactive dashboards.
  • Enabled quick queries using Athena without needing a traditional database engine.

Outcomes
• Built a fully cloud-native data lakehouse that could scale easily with business needs.
• Made it easy to slice and explore large retail datasets without complex setup.
• Helped me understand real-world applications of Spark, S3, and serverless querying.

What I Learned
• How to design a modular pipeline from raw storage to dashboard, end to end.
• How serverless tools like Athena can speed up analytics without high cost.
• Why infrastructure-as-code (Terraform) is essential for reproducibility in cloud projects.

Final Thought
This project taught me how to think like a data engineer — building scalable systems, automating everything, and making data easy to access. Seeing a dashboard light up with insights from millions of rows was honestly the most rewarding part.

Multi-Source Healthcare ETL Framework

Why I Built This
In healthcare, data lives in silos — EMR systems, claims databases, patient CRMs. This fragmentation delays insights and increases the chance of error. I wanted to build an end-to-end ETL pipeline that could bring these sources together and make data more reliable and useful for analytics and care improvement.

Inspiration
I saw how scattered and messy healthcare data can be during my prior projects and case studies. Teams spent more time cleaning and stitching data than actually analyzing it. My goal was to build something reusable and production-ready that would cut through this chaos.

System Architecture
Raw healthcare data (EMR, CRM, and claims) was loaded into BigQuery. dbt was used to transform and clean it into patient-centric models. Airflow handled DAGs for scheduled ingestion and transformation. Snowflake served as the structured warehouse layer where analytics and dashboards could query standardized views.

Tools I Used
• GCP BigQuery to stage raw healthcare records at scale.
• dbt to clean and model data into curated patient, visit, and claims tables.
• Apache Airflow to schedule and orchestrate all extraction and transformation workflows.
• Snowflake to serve clean, analytics-ready data through centralized schemas for reporting.

What It Actually Did

  • Combined 3+ data sources (EMR, CRM, claims) into a unified, queryable warehouse.
  • Created modular dbt models for patient events, visit summaries, and billing cycles.
  • Automated pipeline runs daily using Airflow, ensuring fresh data without manual work.

Outcomes
• Reduced the time to generate patient reports by over 60% for simulated analysts.
• Improved schema standardization — made it easier for downstream teams to work with clean, consistent fields.
• Flagged 1,000+ rows with potential mismatches between EMR and claims datasets.

What I Learned
• How to coordinate multiple ingestion points into one clean pipeline without data loss.
• That dbt really shines in healthcare, where repeatable transformations are critical.
• Why orchestration matters — Airflow kept everything clean, timely, and hands-off.

Final Thought
This project taught me how to bridge messy real-world data and structured analytics systems. It wasn’t just an ETL exercise — it was about helping people get the right answers faster in an industry where timing matters.
